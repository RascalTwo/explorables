What is RAG?

RAG = Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Three plain words: you retrieve the information that's relevant to a question, and use it to augment the generation of an AI's answer. Instead of hoping the answer is baked into the model, you go find it first, then let the model write the reply grounded in what you found.
you already do this by hand

You've already done RAG — manually. Ever found a document, pasted it (or dragged-and-dropped it) into your favorite AI, and asked a question about it? That's RAG. You retrieved something relevant and used it to augment the generation of the answer. You are your own retriever.

The catch: you don't scale. A human can't read 10,000 documents to answer one question, and can't do it in a second, for every question, all day. So we automate and scale that same move — which turns out to be surprisingly hard. That's the problem RAG solves. ↓

why this exists1The problem

People ask an AI questions. The answers aren't in the model's head — they live in your documents (return policy, warranty terms, gear-care guides). That sounds easy until you look closely. Three things make it hard:

The answer is a fragment
It's usually one line of a long document — not the whole file. You want that line, not 40 pages of context.
…spread across several
A real answer ("is wear-and-tear covered?") can stitch together pieces from the warranty doc and the gear-care doc.
won't fit
every doc ≫ context window
…and you can't just load it all
Stuffing every document into the prompt blows the context window, costs a fortune, and buries the answer in noise.
So the job is: given a question, find the few relevant fragments out of a large pile of documents — fast, cheap, and accurate enough to answer from. That's retrieval. Everything below is how you do it well.

Never touched this before? Here's the whole trick, in four short beats:

  • A keyword Ctrl-F can't find meaning: "wear and tear" won't match "damage from normal use" — no shared words.
  • So turn every passage into a vector (an embedding) — numbers that capture its meaning, so text that means the same thing lands nearby.
  • To answer, turn the question into a vector too and grab the passages whose vectors sit closest.
  • Matching by meaning, not words, is why this is also called semantic search.

"Does the box also write the answer?" It depends what "the box" is. Many turnkey RAG solutions ship a full chat assistant — they take the chunks + your question, generate a cited answer, and hand you a web UI, so that dashed "optional ½" comes built in. But a bare RAG engine often just hands back the chunks and leaves the answer to you. Keep the two straight: a turnkey RAG solution may generate the answer for you; a RAG engine usually just retrieves, and your app writes the answer through its own guarded lane. Why you'd make that call is §6 — the most important architectural decision on this page.

open the box3Inside: RAG is really three things

Crack the box open and there are three steps: ingest, retrieve, and generate an answer from what was retrieved. Generation isn't an optional half — it's the real third step. What varies is who owns it: the first two are handled explicitly by the RAG engine; the third is handled by your app (or a higher-level, more-encompassing turnkey RAG solution).

1 · once

Ingestion pipeline

Turn raw documents — any format — into a queryable structure. Runs once, when the corpus is built (or when a doc changes). This is the slow, heavy part.

2 · per question

Retrieval

Take one question, search that structure, and return the top few relevant chunks. Fast. Runs on every ask.

3 · per question

Answer generation — your app, or a turnkey solution

Hand chunks + question to an LLM and write the answer. This step is owned by a different layer — your app, or a turnkey RAG solution — not the retrieval engine (§6).

The rest of this page opens the two engine steps one more level each — ingestion in §4, retrieval in §5 — then §6 covers step three: the one decision, about where answer generation lives, that shapes everything else. The concrete, engine-specific builds live on the sibling pages linked at the end.

the ingestion machine4Ingestion — there is no single "best" way

Newcomers want the right ingestion recipe. There isn't one. The honest answer is metric-driven development: you take real documents and real questions, write evals, try a few recipes, and keep whatever scores best.

That said, almost every recipe is the same five moves — they only differ in the last one, the data structure you land in:

The "good data structures" you can land in

Three are worth knowing. They're not mutually exclusive — you can build all three from the same chunks (we do), and ask different questions of each.

tried & true

Vector index

Store every chunk's embedding. Find answers by vector search (nearest vectors = closest meaning), usually fused with BM25 keyword scoring (decoded in §5 below). The recall baseline. Best for: "find the passage that says X."

whole-document questions

RAPTOR — a summary tree

Cluster related chunks, have an LLM summarise each cluster, then cluster & summarise the summaries — recursively, into a tree. Those synthesised summaries get indexed too. Best for: "give me an overview of…" where the answer is spread across many chunks.

answers between documents

Knowledge graph (GraphRAG)

An LLM extracts entities and the relations between them into a graph. Retrieval can hop along relations across documents. Best for: multi-hop "how does X relate to Y?"

The trick: it's all one vector store. RAPTOR and GraphRAG aren't separate databases you query differently. They just write extra synthetic chunks into the same vector store — RAPTOR upserts a summary chunk for each cluster; GraphRAG upserts an entity/relation record (with an embeddable description). So when a question comes in, one ordinary hybrid search sweeps the raw passages, the summaries, and the relations together — and the best matches win, whatever kind they are. That's why there's no "which engine?" switch — they compose rather than compete.

the retrieval machine5Retrieval — one good question in, top chunks out

RAG answers one question per call. Got 15 things to ask? That's 15 calls — RAG isn't built to take a wall of questions at once. The skill is asking one clear, well-targeted question; the caller (a smart agent) splits big asks into several focused ones.

"dense + sparse, fused" — decoded

Two ways to match a query to a chunk, each blind to what the other catches:

  • Dense = embedding similarity. Matches meaning — "wear and tear" finds "damage from normal use" even with no shared words.
  • Sparse = keyword / BM25. Matches exact terms — a SKU, "warranty", a part number. Dense often fumbles these.
  • Fused / hybrid = combine both scores. You get semantic recall and exact-term precision (see the two figures below).

"rerank" & "top-k" — decoded

Top-k: you don't return everything — you keep the k highest-scoring chunks (k=8 here). Small, focused, fits the prompt.

Rerank: a reranker is a dedicated model that does one job — read each (question, chunk) pair together and re-score it. The first hybrid pass is cheap but noisy; a cross-encoder reranker is slower but much sharper, so it reorders the shortlist before the top-k cut.

Rerank is optional upside: if the first hybrid pass is already good enough, you can leave it off; when you want more precision, you switch it on. Which reranker, and whether it runs at all, is a build-time dial — the concrete choices live on the sibling build pages.

BM25 is classic keyword-overlap scoring — it ranks by the exact words shared between the query and the text, and rare words count most:

Hybrid = run vector + BM25, then fuse the two rankings — a chunk that ranks decently in both searches rises to the top. RRF (reciprocal-rank fusion) is the dead-simple way to merge two ranked lists: each result scores 1 ÷ its rank in each list, you sum the two, and re-sort. Ranking high in both lists wins.

the decision that matters6Why hand back chunks, not an answer?

The box can write the answer (§2). The real choice is where the answering LLM lives — inside the RAG service, or upstream in the app/agent that called it. We chose upstream. Here's the trade:

In plain terms: our RAG service does not call an LLM to answer. It finds the chunks and hands them straight back to your AI app — "here are the relevant documents, you write the answer." The AI's own guarded LLM composes the reply and the citations. The answering brain stays in your app, not buried in the retrieval box. Yes — that's exactly Option B below.
Option A — RAG answers (we rejected)

RAG returns a finished, cited answer.

  • ✅ One call, self-contained. Native citations, ships with a UI.
  • ❌ The answer-writing LLM is buried inside the service — you can't put your guardrails on it.
  • ❌ It's a terminal path: the caller can't reason over the raw evidence, combine it with other tools, or re-ask.
  • ❌ Harder to trace end-to-end — the generation step lives in someone else's box.
Option B — RAG returns chunks (our choice)

RAG is a retrieval tool; the agent/app composes the answer.

  • ✅ The answer is written through our guarded lane, with our citation format, in our trace.
  • ✅ Retrieval is one composable tool in the loop — the agent can call it many times, mix in other tools, and decide.
  • ✅ A RAG outage isn't a 500: the retriever throws, the app notes "retrieval degraded," and the model declines honestly.
  • ❌ More orchestration upstream — we own the answer-composition step (that's the point).
the seam idea In practice this means the whole RAG engine slots in below one method — "given a question, give me the top-k chunks." The app depends on that contract, not on any particular engine. That one seam is what lets you swap engines later without touching the app — the concrete version (the exact method, the client, the wire call) lives on the build pages linked below.
