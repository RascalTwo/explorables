What an attacker has to steal

Every secret in a client-credentials plus JWKS design, placed by how hard it is to get hold of against what it buys whoever gets it. The outline colour says whether you would find out. Two of these are catastrophic, and only one of them is the one people expect.

Mechanism, and every RFC citation, lives in the JWKS explainer and its diagram set. What you actually do when one of these leaks is the companion page: Turning it off. What stops someone simply guessing the client secret in the first place is The failure cap.

Elsewhere — what this page is, in the discipline's own words Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet· OWASP Cheat Sheet Series This map answers two of the four questions that cheat sheet is built around — what can go wrong, and how to rank it — and it ranks the same way, on likelihood against impact. Worth reading for the two questions this page deliberately does not answer: what are we working on (that is a topology diagram, not this) and what are we going to do about it (that is the companion Turning it off). It also names STRIDE, which is the structured sweep to run if you want confidence this map has no holes.
Generic throughout — authorization server, client, resource server. No product names, no network topology. Positions on the map are judgement, not measurement: the axes are ordered bands, not scales, and nothing here is a score.
Where each secret physically sits in a given deployment is a topology question and belongs to that deployment's own network diagram, not to this page.
